### 24.1: Refining your strategies for the black-box optimisation challenge

#### Explain how your understanding of the exploration–exploitation trade-off evolved with increasing data and provide examples of how exploration and exploitation were balanced using data-driven reasoning.
At the start of the project the immediate focus was on exploration to build a better view of the search space, having only on average 5 initial points for each level of dimensionality (2D : 10 points, 3D : 15 points, 6D : 40 points). These initial points were provided as random points (not supported by an acquisition model or as part of an optimisation process). Exploration helped map the areas in the search space with the highest variance and uncertainty. Filling these gaps provided further statistical analysis calculating feature correlations, mutual information, and importance scores revealed that most high-dimensional functions possessed significant redundancy.

This isolated a truer active subspace. The strategy could then safely exploit the high-impact dimensions while focusing its remaining exploration budget exclusively on the critical blind spots within that reduced mapped space. As the project reached the final couple of rounds the strategy shifted to a pure exploitation strategy - removing variance from the acquisition function so that the maxima could be optimised.

The trade-off was ultimately managed by transitioning through three distinct, data-justified structural states:

Phase 1 (Queries 1–4 — Broad Exploration): Target critical blind spots to map global topology and escape local baseline traps.

Phase 2 (Queries 5–10 — Managed Trade-Off): Contract the search space based on dimensional statistics, adjust kernels for non-smoothness, and focus the acquisition function on high-performing clusters.

Phase 3 (Queries 11–13 — Micro-Exploitation): Ignore variance entirely, shifting input parameters by small increments to pinpoint the absolute peak of the identified zones.
 
#### Describe how feedback informed strategic updates and links this process to ideas from Q-learning or MDPs.
As the data set expanded, the nature of feedback shifted from global uncertainty to localised, well performing regions. Initially, feedback was binary and sparse: queries either yielded an uplift or failed. However, as the data set grew, the feedback became more structured, and provided clear signals about the function’s smoothness, noise, and dimensional importance. This evolution in feedback directly mirrors how Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents adjust their reward expectations and update their Q-values within a Markov Decision Process (MDP).
 
#### Reflects on how feedback influenced optimisation efficiency and connects this to RL ideas such as reward updates or delayed rewards
Just as the BO strategy used dimensional statistics to collapse redundant dimensions and focus on active subspaces, an RL agent uses incoming rewards to identify and exploit higher-value state-action paths, effectively ignoring lower-reward areas in the environment.

With a strict budget of only 13 queries, every exploration query carries an opportunity cost. Spending a query on broad exploration in Phase 1 provides structural information that does not lead to an immediate maximum score, but it ensures that later queries are more likely to improve. In Phase 1, the strategy operated with a high implicit discount factor, treating information gain as a valuable long-term asset. By Phase 3, the strategy shifted to a greedy, short-term policy ignoring variance and taking tiny micro-steps to find the localised maxima.

Draw a connection between the iterative improvement and RL approaches, such as self-play or model-free versus model-based learning.
AlphaGo Zero learns entirely through a self-reinforcing iterative loop called policy iteration, starting with zero human knowledge beyond the basic rules of the game. Instead of looking at how humans play, AlphaGo Zero acts as its own teacher. It paired a neural network with a Monte Carlo Tree Search so that the evaluation of the best move kept improving steadily over time. The Bayesian Optimisation process reflected this iterative loop through its query-and-update cadence. Just as AlphaGo Zero discovered strategic concepts purely from data feedback, the BO strategy discovered structural truths about the black-box functions—such as isolating active dimensions.
The optimization process firmly resembled model-based planning rather than model-free trial and error. This strategy used the GP to build a predictive probability distribution across the entire unmapped landscape and given a strict budget of only 13 queries, a model-free trial and error approach would not have been focused enough.
