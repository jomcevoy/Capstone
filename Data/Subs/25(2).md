### 25.2: Successful optimisation strategies

#### Identify optimisation strategies that led to strong results, explain why they were effective and describe how they influenced decision-making during the BBO capstone project.
The optimisation strategies that drove the highest performance spikes during the Black-Box Optimisation (BBO) capstone project relied on three key components; the structured shift from exploration to exploitation; (Scheduling); reducing search space dimensionality (Compression)  and aligning the surrogate model's structure with the rugged topology of the objective functions (Alignment).

**Scheduling :** Keeping exploration static is mathematically proven to be sub-optimal. To guarantee that an optimiser will converge on a true global maximum, the risk profile must be adjusted over time.

**Srinivas et al. (2010)** – Gaussian Process Optimization in the Bandit Setting: This seminal paper introduces the GP-UCB algorithm. It provides the exact mathematical proof for how the exploration hyperparameter must be explicitly scheduled to decay over iterations to achieve "no regret" optimisation.

Rather than keeping the exploration-exploitation balance constant, the project implemented a strategy where risk decayed linearly over time. This strategy optimised the information value of each query relative to the remaining budget:

Early Phase (Queries 1–3): High UCB parameters kappas 3-5; accepted short-term volatility to gather global topological data. This broad net successfully caught the edge of a localized spike in Function 1 and located the global peak for Function 4 on the very first move.

Late Phase (Queries 9–13): Dropping kappa to near-zero and eventually transitioning to local solvers (scipy.optimize.minimize) and continued to squeeze out incremental improvements around the localised maxima. This transition successfully drove peak scores for Functions 1, 3, 7, and 8 in the final query.
 
**Compression :** Real-world high-dimensional functions often possess a low intrinsic dimensionality—meaning only a fraction of the inputs actively drive the output change. Compressing the search space prevents the optimiser from getting lost in vast, inert coordinate valleys.

**Eriksson et al. (2019)** – Scalable Global Optimization via Local Bayesian Optimisation: This paper introduces TuRBO (Trust Region Bayesian Optimization). Instead of searching the global space, it dynamically compresses the active search area into localised "trust regions" that shrink or expand based on success.

This was the most impactful optimisation strategy was manually bounding or freezing dimensions based on structural and statistical reviews of feature importance.

Under the strict 13-query limit, high-dimensional spaces suffer severely from the curse of dimensionality. For example, in Function 7 (Hyperparameters), statistical profiling revealed that a single primary feature commanded 81% of the total model importance and exerted a heavy negative correlation on the target output.

By identifing this behaviour and permanently binding that feature to zero, the effective search space was collapsed. This eliminated a massive blind spot and concentrated the remaining query budget entirely on the variables that could actively drive improvements, resulting in an output that doubled the original baseline.
 
**Alignment :** If there is a "model mismatch"—such as using an infinitely smooth kernel to map a rough, jagged physical landscape—the optimizer's calculations of local gradients will be entirely wrong, leading to complete optimisation failure.

**Snoek, Larochelle, & Adams (2012)** – Practical Bayesian Optimization of Machine Learning Algorithms: the landmark paper that convinced the ML community to stop defaulting to the over-smooth Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel. They argue extensively that real-world ML loss surfaces are rugged and non-differentiable, making the Matern 5/2 kernel the mandatory baseline choice for proper topological alignment.

Early runs assumed the response surfaces were smooth, utilising a standard Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel. Weeks 4–8 saw a deliberate pivot to a Matern covariance kernel with adjusted noise parameters. Real-world black-box landscapes are rarely continuous, gently rolling hills. Functions like Function 6 (Cake Function) were highly jagged and non-differentiable. The RBF kernel was overconfident, smoothing over localized peaks.

The Matern kernel explicitly accounted for this roughness, while the increased noise alpha prevented the surrogate model from over-fitting to deceptive local spikes. This immediately stabilised the search loop and allowed the optimiser to find a stable local maximum in Function 6.


#### Clearly articulate what makes a strategy ‘successful’ (e.g. outcomes, adaptability, reasoning, efficiency), supported by reasoning.
In black-box optimisation a strategy success needs to be defined by more than the maximisation objective function outputs **(What)**. Success is also defined by the effectiveness / efficiency of the process **(How)** and the interpretability of the results **(Why)**.

Here are four key measures of a successful strategy.

**Outcomes :** Delivering a global maximum or reaching a target threshold within the allocated query budget. However a strategy that safely navigates a highly volatile, negative-yielding space to achieve an incremental maximum is often more successful than one that hits an easy peak in a smooth, trivial landscape. 
**Adaptability (effectiveness) :** Static optimisation models struggle when faced with complex, unknown environments. True strategic success requires dynamic feedback, such that if a  loop encounters unexpected noise or a sharp local cliff, an adaptable strategy shifts its hyperparameters (like decaying Kappa or adjusting a trust region).
**Efficiency :** With infinite time and resources, even a random search can find a global maximum. Therefore, optimisation success is tied to sample efficiency - maximising the information gain and score improvement achieved per query. A strategy is more successful when the information gathered through exploration provides the insight for the exploitation phase to be successful. The efficiency is also tied to dimension reduction - simplifying the problem makes the process more efficient.
**Reasoning (interpretability) :** A high score achieved through random exploration is a lucky accident, not a sign of a repeatable successful strategy (although we do explore with the possibility of hitting large random gains . Strategic reasoning ensures that every coordinate choice can be defended by statistical backup . A strategy built on structured priors and explicit covariance calculations, can be documented, automated, and successes can be repeated in other projects.
 
#### Connect successful optimisation strategies to real-world ML/AI contexts, explaining how they could be adapted or applied beyond the BBO capstone project.
The core strategies highlighted as part of this exercise —Scheduling, Compression, and Alignment—are just as important to real-world ML/AI contexts where every single query can carry large financial costs or strict time constraints. Executing these strategies could be the difference between a failed project and a successful deployment.

In the capstone, Scheduling meant adjusting the acquisition hyperparameter kappa to shift from broad exploration to clinical local search. Beyond capstone in a real-world context a good example of scheduling is the Hyperband Algorithm. Early in the training process (Exploration), the algorithm allows diverse, risky parameter combinations to run. As the budget burns out, hyperband acts as an aggressive, automated project manager that cuts funding to failing experiments and funnels those savings into higher performing experiments. 

In the capstone, Compression involved using Automated Relevance Determination (ARD) to drop inert dimensions and manually build bounding boxes around high-importance features. In the real world pruning is a very common technique in a lot of ML/AI projects. In a LLM millions of neurons and connections contribute absolutely nothing to the final output for 99% of user prompts. They are essentially dead weight. During deployment preparation, engineers run a sensitivity analysis that tracks which neural pathways remain dark or unchanged across a diverse set of test queries. The algorithm then permanently severs and removes those inert connections. The active structural footprint of the network reduces making it more efficient.

Finally in the capstone, Alignment meant understanding the ruggedness of the search space and swapping a RBF kernel for a Matern kernel with tuned noise alphas to match the jagged reality of the functions. In the real world ML/AI processes are designed to expect non linear functions and underlying noise. The ‘kernal trick’ is used in Support Vector Machines (SVM) and in PCA to raise the function into a space where the non-linear relationships are transformed into a linear structure. Similarly with noise in real world ML/AI there are a number of tools to reduce the impact of noise effect on the core algorithm; including regularisation and bagging.
