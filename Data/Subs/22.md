#### Have you identified any ‘clusters’ or recurring regions in your search space that seem promising Describes clear clusters or recurring regions (or justifies their absence) and explains reasoning using evidence from query results
Once the initial exploration phase had been completed (queries 1-4 or 5) the next phase resulted in a number of useful clusters as the acquisition function traded towards targeted exploration and exploitation. The acquisition function looks at high-performing points, factors in the surrounding uncertainty, and determines that the "value of information" is highest right next to that point. This creates a feedback loop and the algorithm repeatedly samples the same localised zone, forming a dense cluster of query points as it maps out the boundaries of the peak.

This can be seen in the results to date - in the low dimensional functions the acquisition function has discovered high-reward zones and repeatedly samples tightly packed coordinates around it. The fact that performance kept rising proves that these clusters represent a true maxima, rather than statistical anomalies. In the higher dimensional functions active bounding and length scale tightening have accentuated the clustering.

#### Which strategies or parameter choices have proven less effective, and how are you adjusting for them? Identifies ineffective strategies or parameters and outlines specific adjustments or next steps
In the early rounds the simplified GP acquisition functions were less effective in exploration. For low dimensional functions they tended to identify the highest areas of variance on the boundaries or in the corners; which reduced the effective information gathered. To combat this I reduce the length scales to encourage smaller movements. 

For the higher dimensional functions the simplified functions were also less effective as points naturally sit incredibly far apart, meaning standard exploration strategies struggle to build an accurate surrogate model map. Here we utilised feature correlations, mutual information, and importance scores to isolate the dominant dimensions. By fixing the less impactful dimensions to their historical best-performing constants, we effectively compressed the high-dimensional functions.

The simplified models also suffered from not having a noise variance parameter (alpha). Because they assume zero noise, the GP is mathematically forced to become an exact interpolator forcing the mean prediction curve to violently bend and twist to pass precisely through every point. Increasing the parameter from zero to a small fraction on particularly the higher dimensional functions allows the model to remain highly sensitive to sharp changes, but injecting just a tiny bit of noise variance prevents the GP from generating artificial, ”cliffs" when a query drops slightly.
 
#### In what ways do your refinements parallel how clustering algorithms separate meaningful patterns from noise Draws a clear analogy between optimisation refinements and clustering concepts (e.g. pattern recognition, filtering noise)
The dimension reduction in the BBO would parallel to running Principal Component Analysis (PCA) before clustering. In high-dimensional spaces. In high-dimensional spaces, data points naturally drift incredibly far apart and clustering can be difficult to do meaningfully. 

The noise parameter in the acquisition function is analogous to the DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) which do not force every point into a cluster. Any data point that does not meet the minimum density threshold (i.e., it lacks enough close neighbours within a specified radius, or epsilon) is explicitly flagged as a noise point and left unclustered

Finally the reduced length scale is similar to how cluster analysis converges on centroids; starting with large steps to generate the initial clusters which are refined to smaller changes as the cluster approaches convergence.
