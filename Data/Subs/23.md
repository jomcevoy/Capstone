### 23.1: Refining your strategies for the black-box optimisation challenge

#### How has your optimisation strategy evolved since your first few rounds of queries? Which elements now feel more structured or systematic? 
The overall strategy has naturally fallen into three phases; Because we already possessed the initial data sets, we could immediately construct a simple surrogate model (Gaussian Process).

The first few rounds Phase 1 - Exploration :  in the exploration phase we encourage the algorithm to target critical blind spots—especially large unmapped gaps sitting right next to high-performing historical data points. Spending any more query budget on exploration, (particularly in the high dimensional functions) would likely have been unproductive.

Phase 2 of the optimisation has involved refining each of the models to fit the specific challenges thrown up by each of the functions.

Function 1 - Radiation : once we had found a localised spike, creating a proximity radius allowed the model to search the edges of the spike -  and find its peak.

Function 2 - Log-Likelihood :  experimented with a pytorch model to avoid getting stuck in local maxima by using the qlogEI in the acquisition function.

Function 3 - Drug Discovery : the statistical analysis highlighted a dominant negative dimensional and a secondary dimension whose impact is heaving reliant on the dominant dim. Modelling restricts the bounds on these two dimensions to encourage better expected improvement.  

Function 4 - Dynamic / Noisy : early gains but exploration hampered by deep valleys. Bounds across all 4 dimensions to keep search close to the local maxima.

Function 5 - Chemical Process :  results continue to show improvement towards the corner of the search space. Again bounds across all 4 dimensions to keep search close to the local maxima.

Function 6 - Cake Recipe :  the statistical analysis highlighted a dominant positive dimensional and a secondary dimension with a high negative correlation - penalty trap. Focus on iterative small moves along these dimensions.

Function 7 - Six Hyperparameters : has a hugely dominant negative dimension which has caused inconsistent results. Once we implemented an anisotropic kernel (ARD) the performance has improved steadily.

Function 8 - Sparse - Local Maxima : statistical analysis highlighted two dominant dimensions and from these a local maxima has been selected and exploited.

#### Thinking of the 8D Bayesian Optimisation through the lens of Principal Component Analysis (PCA), we can map the features directly to the concept of variance explanation.
In PCA, the first principal component (PC1) accounts for the absolute maximum variance in the data, while subsequent components capture secondary structural variations or drop off into negligible noise. In this example PC1  would be a combination of input_S and input_U, accounting for 77.5% of the variance and both heavily negatively correlated to the output. #### How do you decide which aspects of your strategy to keep exploring versus which to reduce or simplify, as PCA reduces dimensions while retaining essential information? 
In a high-dimensional optimisation function, deciding what to explore versus what to simplify is a balancing act between information preservation and query budget.
Using the framework of Principal Component Analysis (PCA), where we project data onto axes of maximum variance and drop the rest, we can establish clear rules for what to keep, what to lock down, and what to compress. By applying this PCA mindset, we can effectively transform a highly complex 8-Dimensional search space into a focused 3-Dimensional bounded space.

#### Reflect briefly on how insights from PCA, such as focusing on variance and removing redundancy, might apply to how you interpret your BBO results. 
PCA teaches us that in high-dimensional datasets, most of the variance (the "information" the data is trying to tell) is usually concentrated in just a few directions and instead of trying to analyse dozens or hundreds of variables at once, PCA allows us to simplify the problem by focusing only on the coordinates that matter most. PCA helps us think about optimisation outcomes as a series of geometric compressions, in effect bringing the landscape into focus.

Highly correlated inputs provide overlapping information and this redundant duplication is removed in PCA (through weightings) or via length scales in BBO.
